Distinguishing between moderation and mediation

Distinguishing between moderation and mediation

Diagram of mediation design

What type of research question?

Mediation is an analysis technique. However, we use it to answer the following type of research question:

To what extent is the relationship between X and Y mediated by another variable (M)?

Consider the following example:

A researcher is interested in the relationship between the treatment a person receives and their mental health. They hypothesise that the relationship between treatment and mental health is mediated by the extent to which they practice the techniques they are taught in therapy, in their everyday life.

Diagram of mediation design

What is the difference between mediation and moderation?

  • Mediation is theorising about the mechanism by which an effect occurs.

  • Moderation is theorising about the conditions under which an effect changes.

That is, from a theoretical perspective:

Mediation is hypothesising about why something happens in a relationship, and suggesting that the relationship can be explained through the mediator variable.

Moderation is hypothesising that a relationship is different under certain conditions (i.e. the changing level of the moderator variable).

How do we test for mediation?

  • There are a number of ways to test for mediation.

    • The “classic” approach is to use the Baron and Kenny (1986) approach.
    • The “modern” approach is to use the Preacher and Hayes (2008) approach.

  • It is worth understanding both approaches, as they are both used in the literature.

  • However, the Preacher and Hayes approach is more robust and more flexible.

Baron and Kenny (1986) approach to mediation

Baron and Kenny (1986) approach to mediation

  • The classic Baron and Kenny (1986) approach uses a series of regression steps to evaluate paths between \(X\), \(M\), and \(Y\):

    1. Show that \(X\) predicts \(Y\) (Total effect, Path \(c\)).
    2. Show that \(X\) predicts \(M\) (Path \(a\)).
    3. Show that \(M\) predicts \(Y\) when controlling for \(X\) (Path \(b\)).
    4. Check whether the direct effect of \(X\) on \(Y\) (Path \(c'\)) drops to non-significance (full mediation) or is reduced in size (partial mediation) when \(M\) is included.

  • The Sobel test was traditionally used to test the significance of the indirect effect (\(a \times b\)).

Limitations of the Baron and Kenny approach

  • Modern methodologists no longer require a statistically significant total effect (Path \(c\)) before testing mediation (Hayes, 2009; Zhao et al., 2010).

  • In inconsistent mediation (suppression), the direct effect (\(c'\)) and indirect effect (\(a \times b\)) have opposite signs and cancel each other out, yielding a non-significant total effect despite genuine mediation.

  • Rather than relying on separate significance tests, modern approaches directly estimate and test the indirect effect (\(a \times b\)).

If we assume that we have the following data:

  • X is the independent variable (treatment)
  • M is the mediator (practice)
  • Y is the dependent variable (mental health)

Then in the classic framework we examine:

  1. X is related to Y (Path c total effect)
  2. X is related to M (Path a)
  3. M is related to Y (Path b)
  4. The relationship between X and Y is reduced when M is included in the model (Path c’ direct effect)

Step 3.1: Run the mediation analysis on the models

The mediate function gives us:

  • Average Causal Mediation Effects (ACME = indirect effect, \(a \times b\))
  • Average Direct Effects (ADE = direct effect, Path \(c'\))
  • combined indirect and direct effects (Total Effect, Path \(c = c' + ab\))
  • the ratio of these estimates (Prop. Mediated = \(ab / c\)).

The ACME here is the indirect effect of M (total effect - direct effect) and thus this value tells us if our mediation effect is significant.

plot(fitMed)

Step 3.2: Plot the mediation analysis of the models

The plot reiterates what was on the previous slide:

  • The confidence intervals of Total Effect and ACME are significant
  • The confidence interval of ADE is not significant

Translation:

  • Total effect is significant: there is a relationship between X and Y (direct and indirect)
  • ADE is not significant: the direct relationship between X and Y (controlling for M) is not significant
  • ACME is significant: the relationship between X and Y is mediated by M

Bootstrapping

Bootstrapping the mediation model

  • In mediation::mediate(), the default is boot = FALSE (a quasi-Bayesian Monte Carlo approximation). Setting boot = TRUE runs non-parametric bootstrapping.

  • Bootstrapping is a more robust method of estimating confidence intervals for the indirect effect than the Sobel test.

  • Bootstrapping is a simulation method that involves repeatedly sampling from the data with replacement (typically 5,000 times).

  • The confidence intervals are then estimated empirically from the distribution of the bootstrapped indirect effects (\(a \times b\)).

Why use bootstrapping?

  • Non-parametric bootstrapping does not assume the indirect effect is normally distributed, providing more accurate confidence intervals and higher statistical power (Preacher & Hayes, 2008).

  • Important caveat: Standard bootstrapping still relies on the underlying OLS models (fitM and fitY), so it does not protect against outliers or heteroscedasticity—we still need to check model diagnostics.

  • Bootstrapping is not just for mediation analysis; it can be used across many statistical analyses to estimate confidence intervals.

Study design and power considerations

Study design and causal assumptions

  • Temporal precedence: From a theoretical perspective, mediation implies a causal sequence (\(X \rightarrow M \rightarrow Y\)). However, cross-sectional mediation (all variables measured at one time point) can produce biased estimates of longitudinal processes (Maxwell & Cole, 2007).

  • Controlling for confounds : Even in an RCT where \(X\) is randomized, \(M\) is measured rather than manipulated—so the \(M \rightarrow Y\) path remains correlational and vulnerable to unmeasured confounders (Imai et al., 2010).

  • Practical takeaway: Mediation is not a substitution for longitudinal designs. Control variables or baseline scores can be included as covariates in both fitM and fitY models (similar to multiple regression approach).

Sample size and statistical power

  • Like moderation, mediation requires careful a priori power analysis.

  • To achieve 80% power when both paths (\(a\) and \(b\)) are small-to-moderate can require \(N \approx 400+\) participants (Fritz & MacKinnon, 2007).

  • When planning studies, check sample size feasibility early using Monte Carlo mediation power calculators (e.g., Schoemann et al.’s mc_power_med).

Mediation analysis with a categorical mediator

Mediation analysis with a categorical mediator

  • The mediation package can also be used to test for mediation with a categorical mediator or predictor.

  • The approach is the same as above, but the regression models will specify the mediator or predictor as a factor variable.

Mediation analysis with binary outcomes

  • It is also possible to test mediation if your outcome is binary, for example (e.g., Yes / No).

  • This will mean that some of the regression models will be logistic regression models, like this (glm is used for logistic regression):

model <- glm(Outcome ~ Predictor, family = "binomial", data = myData)
